## 1. Search: Claude’s objection is right

I overstated search as the best channel. A search for “hide photos of my ex” establishes a problem, not willingness to pay $100. A useful guide may satisfy the reader without creating a customer—and that’s fine.

**My hypothesis, not evidence:** someone might pay for the confidence of a complete, recoverable process across several places, plus the emotional permission to put things away without deciding forever. That value must survive an honest explanation that much of the work is manual and some apps are inaccessible.

I’d publish **one** useful guide, not four, and distribute it where permitted. In parallel, recruit interviewees directly. Judge search by qualified purchase attempts and the time spent generating them, not traffic or signups alone.

## 2. Isolating Go Back: test it, but counterbalance the order

Claude’s proposal is directionally sound. The weakness is that adding Go Back second can create an artificial “improvement”: people may react positively simply because they’ve been given more.

For 10–15 interviews:

- Randomly show half a cleanup-only concept first, then the complete offer; reverse the order for the other half. Neither is presented as a product available to buy.
- After each, ask what they would actually do instead and whether they would pursue it at **$100**. Ask what changed and why.
- For the complete offer, disclose that restoration costs extra and requires the ex-held code. Ask them to explain those conditions back.
- Test **$29, $49 and $79** as separate possible Go Back prices across participants; ask whether each changes their interest in the initial $100 purchase.

This is exploratory: a few interviews won’t establish a winning price or causal effect. It can reveal whether Go Back attracts buyers, causes concern, or is misunderstood.

## 3. “Buy” button: stronger than a signup, weaker than a sale

Yes—a click at a clearly displayed $100 is more meaningful than an email signup. It is still cheap intent: no payment details, commitment or actual purchase.

I’d label the button **“Try to buy for $100”** or **“I’d buy for $100”**, with **“Not available yet—no payment will be taken”** beside it. The next screen can offer an optional early-access signup. Disclose the separate Go Back charge and ex-held code before the click; if the price isn’t set, say so rather than implying it is $49.

Count unique visitors, offer views, purchase-intent clicks and optional signups. Avoid collecting the ex’s email, relationship details or intimate content. Confirm privacy disclosures and applicable advertising/consumer-law requirements before publishing; don’t imply the product exists or that vault feasibility is proven. A web demand test is not an App Store purchase flow, but Apple’s payment and entitlement rules need review before an iPhone app sells unlocks.

## 4. Gifting: a small interview question, not a launch channel

The friend-as-buyer idea is plausible, not established. It also has two conversion hurdles: the giver must pay, and the recipient must want and complete the process. A breakup gift could feel intrusive or pressure someone toward a choice they haven’t made.

Ask interviewees how they would feel receiving it and ask a few potential givers what they have actually bought for a friend in that situation. Don’t build gifting or spend a separate channel budget yet. If tested later, **only the recipient** should enter the ex’s email and initiate setup; check platform gifting and purchase rules first.

The code-email flow also needs legal and safety review: an unsolicited email can worry a recipient or be abused. The owner still needs to decide what happens when delivery fails or the ex declines to share the code.

## Recommended 90-day plan

1. **Days 1–15: Define the offer and economics.** Owner decides what the $100 covers. Draft honest copy and a contribution model including fees, refunds, support, acquisition and maintenance time. No customer money.
2. **Days 1–35: Run the vault spike.** With test files, verify storage, confirmed removal, second-device restoration and failures: storage full, lost device/account access and app removal. Document what remains unproven, especially years-long recoverability.
3. **Days 15–55: Interview 10–15 people.** Use the counterbalanced offer test; check comprehension, alternatives and Go Back price reactions. Ask a few gifting questions.
4. **Days 25–80: Run one landing-page test.** Publish the complete offer and honest intent button. Share one genuinely useful guide and the page through permitted, relevant channels. Record acquisition time as well as the funnel.
5. **Days 80–90: Decide.** Review feasibility, comprehension, demand and economics together. If promising, obtain owner decisions and legal/platform review before committing to a limited build or accepting payment.

## Decision thresholds

These are **working gates, not statistically reliable forecasts**:

- **Build a limited first release:** spike succeeds without an unresolved data-loss or restoration blocker; at least **8 of 10** interviewees can explain the manual limits, separate charge and ex-held code; at least **4 of 10** would pursue the complete offer at $100; and **8 of 100 unique offer visitors** click purchase intent, with at least **3** leaving contact details. Check for duplicate or bot traffic.
- **Change the explanation, acquisition or implementation:** meaningful interest exists but comprehension, funnel or feasibility misses a gate. Retest one major change. If fewer than 100 visitors arrive, treat demand as **inconclusive**, not a pass.
- **Stop this approach:** a fundamental feasibility, safety or legal blocker remains unresolved; or after a revision and another reasonable recruitment effort, people consistently prefer free alternatives and purchase intent remains well below the gate. That is a decision to stop investing in this approach, not proof nobody would buy.

For economic context: at an illustrative **$60 contribution per sale**, $500 annual fixed costs and **10 operating/acquisition hours a month valued at $40/hour** require about **89 sales a year** to cover those costs. Build time remains additional. None of those inputs is validated.

## Remaining disagreement

Claude favors search as an acquisition candidate; I now regard it as **one cheap experiment**, not the default growth plan. We agree Go Back’s pull deserves isolation, but I would counterbalance the interview order rather than always introduce it second. Finally, even if the numbers pass, I’d authorize only a limited build—not assume the landing-page clicks predict profitable sales.
