## Independent assessment

**Overall:** The emotional idea is strong: “I don’t have to see this now, and I don’t have to decide it’s gone forever.” The product’s biggest challenge is delivering that promise across fragmented apps and storage without making recovery sound more certain than it is.

### 1. Emotional value and the $100 price

**Evidence:** Demand at $100 is unvalidated. The work spans more than photos, and some of it cannot be automated by an iPhone app.

**My view:** $100 could be justified as a guided, private transition—not as a photo-cleaning utility. But the customer must understand exactly what they receive: which sources the service helps with, what they must do themselves, where copies live, and what the separate Go Back charge means. “Make reminders disappear” is compelling; “we found and secured everything” would be an unsafe promise.

### 2. Manual-first versus AI-assisted

**My recommendation:** Start by testing a guided manual experience. It can address places automated discovery cannot reach and avoids making face recognition a prerequisite. It also lets customers choose what matters, rather than treating every image of a person as something to remove.

The risk is that manual work feels like paying $100 for a checklist. The experience needs clear progress, practical source-by-source instructions, and confidence about what has—and hasn’t—been secured. Optional AI may later reduce effort, but its coverage, platform permissions, accuracy, and biometric legal implications need validation first.

### 3. Go Back’s influence on the first purchase

Go Back may make cleanup emotionally possible: customers can act today without feeling they are making an irreversible declaration. That value exists even if they never restore anything.

The ex-held code is unconventional, but it serves the deliberate two-person ritual. The real obstacles are delivery, loss, refusal to share, and safety: contacting an ex may be distressing or inappropriate in some relationships. Explain the mechanism and its limits **before purchase**, including that the ex receives an email and that restoration costs extra. A “meaningless code” contains no relationship details, but the email’s sender or context could still lead the recipient to infer something.

### 4. Repeat-cycle revenue

Treat it as upside, not a forecast. Some couples reunite and separate again, but there is no evidence here that they will repeat a $100 cleanup or pay to restore. Even a successful product could see low Go Back usage because the *option* provides value without being exercised. Validate the first purchase before modeling repeat revenue.

### 5. Experience ideas

- **Name the chapter**, privately and optionally. Give the process a beginning without forcing a story.
- **Build a source map:** Photos, downloads, message attachments, shared albums and cloud storage. Let customers mark each as secured, deliberately left alone, or still to review.
- **Seal with an honest receipt:** where the vault lives, what was placed in it, what remains elsewhere, and what is required for Go Back. Don’t label unchecked sources “clean.”
- **Create a quiet stopping point:** “You can leave the rest for another day,” rather than a streak or completion pressure.

### 6. Feasibility and uncertainty

**Feasible in principle now:** Guided instructions, customer-selected files, a protected vault, code generation and email, and a paid unlock. Each still needs implementation and security testing.

**Real constraints:** An iPhone app cannot directly read other apps’ private messages. Photos deletion requires system confirmation and leaves items in Recently Deleted temporarily. Google Photos access is limited to user-picked items. Removing a reminder from one place may not remove copies, shared items or synced versions elsewhere.

**Unresolved:** A vault that remains recoverable for years across device loss, cloud-account changes and app updates; reliable code delivery and lockout handling; AI coverage and legal compliance; and the applicable app-store purchase rules. Vault location is a product choice, but reliable long-term recovery is an engineering and operational obligation.

## Cheapest test—and the likeliest wrong assumption

**Test:** Publish one simple landing page describing the manual-first offer at **$100**, the ex-held code, and Go Back as separately paid with its price not yet set. Invite interested visitors to request early access; collect no payment. Share it through a few relevant, permission-based channels and track visits and requests. Genuine requests would justify deeper interviews and a prototype; they would **not** prove willingness to pay. Low traffic alone would not disprove demand.

**Most likely wrong assumption:** That manual and AI-assisted routes can reach the same comprehensive result. Both can help customers put selected memories away, but inaccessible messages, duplicates and shared content make complete coverage uncertain. Design and sell around the result actually achievable.
